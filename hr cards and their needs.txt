Issues re Card Removal:

- "The villain being beyond normal reach" is an essential card, at least in some form.
	- Even if that reach is only beyond the reach of the Hai (including Hai intelligence operatives, assassins, political agents, or hired guns). Otherwise it would all be resolved without the player.
		- So it has to be beyond the reach of the Hai (and all possible agents of the Hai).

- Elenchus is not a card on its own. The whole point is that there's a pirate/smuggler/unsavoury organisation behind things, operating from somewhere (Elenchus) that is beyond normal reach of the Hai.
	-> Elenchus is the card that answers the question of How [pirate card] and [beyond normal reach] cards interact. If we’re borrowing cards terms; it completes the straight flush.
		- So it’s not actually a move away from the pirates, not really.
	- I see the issue that Elenchus being what it is then necessitates the additional card of:
		- "how and why does this exist separately"
			- Which is what pulls in Terry and the Syndicate infiltration.
			- So that’s a sizeable hand of cards to draw from it.
				- If you take Terry out though then you’ve gotta answer the question of why the Hai gov (if they could trace something to human space at least) couldn’t pressure human govs to look into it, since no authority in the Syndicate would have reason to provide any interference to that process.
				- And at that point we’re unpicking most of the story…
					- (Because if Humans could reach it normally when Hai couldn’t, we circle back around to agents of the Hai [diplomatically leveraging the Republic in this case] being able to do something, and negating the need for the player.)

- The hyperspace lore only exists insofar that it satisfies the beyond normal reach requirement, but it's also self-justified in itself:
	- It intrinsically plays on the nature of corporate and government safety regulations coupled with centralized long term information storage.
	- There *are* links out there that fall outside the bounds of what mass produced (and poorly understood) HDs *will* access, but which those same HDs *can* access if safety settings are circumvented, and information on that was just never preserved/advertised, because it’s not safe OR profitable from a corporate or government perspective.
		- Also; we already rely on hidden and new systems for advancing plots and missions, so any lore that justifies its usage in a specific aspect should be acceptable as long as it makes sense within the presented context in-universe. Simply rejecting the use of hidden systems because someone *feels* a certain lore angle doesn’t itself work is deeply unreasonable;
			- an actual objection over the lore itself should come with changes or alternatives that would *allow* the lore to work.
	- ALTERNATIVE: Use #8621 to have an outfit that the smugglers use to control access to the system.
	- ALTERNATIVE: Also, some people really like the deep space systems, but we could remove them and have Elenchus be directly in the hidden system - but it's still gotta be hidden.

- The inclusion of the Korath.
	- It is the Korath amongst them which allow the kidnappers to have a technological edge, combined with hai tech.
		- And that technological edge justifies the whole conspiracy in it's modern form.
			- Without it, there's really just no reason at all for Terry (the Syndicate lady) to participate in the kidnappings or interact at all with the pirate group there.
			- Because hai tech they can get their hands on relatively easily - in fact that's likely how they got into this whole business in the first place, breadcrumbs followed from illicitly acquiring Hai tech.
	- Additionally, it's an alternative glimpse into Korath society/culture as a parallel to the other work on the Korath. Their inclusion on Elenchus helps highlight the short generation time -and- the consequences of being separated from a larger group’s passed on knowledge and mores.
		- It’s also a good counterpoint to pirate campaign play -and- the earlier pirate work in HR… another example of how oppressive societies carry on momentum through changing leadership… so simple top down displacement is not effective, like in violent resolutions to Scar’s Legion (which is already lightly addressed there by current leader not being “Scar”).




- KEY CONCLUDING/Reference POINT
	- Ultimately, the story needs to have a reason for the player to be involved.
		- The political situation could *already* be resolved, eventually, without the player's involvement.
			- This is, to some degree, the point. The player is a small but significant part in it. They affect the specific nature of the outcome, but not the broad strokes of it.
		- The player's significance is a direct result of:
			- Being reasonably famous.
			- Having trust with and access to high-ranking people in a human government.
			- Having access to jump drives.
			- Being competent enough to travel effectively wherever they need to.
		- If the story does not use anything that leans on these points of significance, then the player's involvement is not really significant.
			- If we take out the only thing that makes the player's involvement significant, then there's no reason for the player to be there at all.
			- Especially, since the three most significant governments involved all have competent intelligence agencies.

Old Smuggler PR Essays:
- UnorderedSigh:
	- Re the significance of the smugglers to Hai/Human relations:
	The entire purpose of 5 & 6, and the later part of 4, is to explore the growing connection between the humans, Unfettered, and Fettered, by presenting a common problem that they had to solve together and forging a relationship in the process. It could have degraded into a warfare, but it didn't, because Captain Last helped leaders like Teeneep, Alondo, Remington, and Choot'k understand each other's civilizations. None of the three groups had the technology, experience, or resources to tackle it on their own. They had to unite and create a relationship to solve it. This trial by fire is how and why the human and Hai relationship came to be. By putting the player in the middle of it, and having them drive the story, it becomes more real because Captain Last forges the Hai-human relationship, instead of just reading about it.
	
	Elenchus and the Whispering Void, an unsettling region of space filled with unknown threats, is critical to this, as the player is delving into unfamiliar territory, just like the Hai and humans relations. Captain Last is feeling the fear and uncertainty that people are, all over Hai and human space, confronting a new era of two species united. As the Captain progresses further, seeing the miracles and wonder beyond this terrifying frontier, this gives them the same feeling that the Hai and humans have after sharing cultures, goods, and experiences.
	
	You could replace this with a mile of dry text that "explores" the relationship between humans and Hai without creating one in the first place, but the story would be weaker. This was painstaking work of several people over the course of two and a half years to establish and demonstrate that relationship, and build the lore behind it. Replacing it with the work of two weeks to explore a relationship that wouldn't exist, will not give you a better result. Having a deep story with creative settings isn't necessarily a bad thing. Letting the player forge the Hai-human relationship is a more powerful story than having them read about it.

- NomadicVolcano:
	- Re the Smuggler arc (cont.):
	The smuggler story arc is the most critical moment of player engagement in all of Hai Reveal. This is the moment when nobody in the galaxy except Captain Last can provide a path to peace (G: that *also* satisfies the mystery). It changes the story of forging a Hai-human relationship from merely being facilitated by Captain Last, to being impossible without the Captain.
	
	The normally passive Fettered Hai were so upset by the kidnappings that they elected the youngest "Elder" in 10,000 years, sparked an election in weeks that replaced a third of the Elder Council, compared Teeneep to a messiah from the Mythos (that's new!), and considered allying with the Unfettered in war against the humans. A few Hai know Sayari's details about the kidnapping operation, and are terrified of the explosive consequences of letting it out. Fleets on either side of the wormhole have guns pointed at each other with fingers on the trigger. The leaders know that more stories of Hai abuse will lead to war.
	
	It is impossible to achieve peace until the 5000 Hai are found (G: or conclusively *not* found), and that can't be done until Captain Last boards kidnapping vessels, causing Hai to die, in a quest to get information. Nobody can do that except Captain Last. If the human governments do it, they risk sparking a war by directly killing Hai. The Hai can't do it without sparking a war by sending warships deep into human space. Ordinary mercenaries and bounty hunters can't be trusted with something so sensitive. I don't think I need to mention the Syndicate. Literally, the fates of two entire species are riding on Captain Last using the smugglers to finding Devil-Run without any other help.
	
	If you remove this, Captain Last is no longer the hero of the story; they're just a minor character along for the ride. You must understand what you're changing, and how it links together, before you rewrite a deep and intricate interactive story. Otherwise, a few weeks of work will just look like... a few weeks of work.


- UnorderedSigh:
	- Re the threat of war being "contrived":
	No, eternal stability of the Hai during rising internal and external problems is contrived; Hai Reveal 3 & 4 replaced that with a more realistic scenario: when everyone's problems get too large, and a traumatic event is shoved into everyone's faces, populists can take over and send things into unstable directions. Sometimes they improve society, and sometimes they don't, depending on where the chaotic situation leads. This has happened over and over throughout human history. There's no reason why Hai would be immune; just look at their violent imperialist history and Unfettered brethren.
	
	The Hai nearly went to war with humans - or, to be more precise, the Unfettered on their behalf - because Teeneep and her passionate movement stirred them up into a fury. The reason it was so easy to stir them up is that that people were fed up with their impotent Elders who have been ignoring the Hai troubles for centuries in favor of secrecy and stability. The Hai have been suffering from a toxic stagnation for a long time. It led to income disparities, unchecked pirate troubles, inability to help humans with their own problems, threats to Hai in human space, alienating a large segment of their population (the Unfettered), among other problems. Each segment of the population had their own grievances, and it built up to a dangerous level.
	
	People have been getting increasingly upset, and the pirate troubles that started after the Pug War were the flash point. Teeneep and Choot'k, being populists, seized the opportunity to grab power and fix the problems. Teeneep's ease into power was made possible largely because other Elders and high-ranking Hai had been pushing for change for a very long time. (Especially Sayari and Aimet Taepip.) The same wave that brought Teeneep into power also changed the balance of power in the Elder Council, allowing progress for the first time in, perhaps, millennia. Ultimately, war did not happen because leaders on both sides ensured it didn't, and they had Captain Last to help them along.
	
	Put simply: the childish dichotomy of the utopian and dystopian Hai factions is gone. Hai have a deeper story, a real personality, well-developed lore, and the actions of their population are based on how actual populations evolve. If you remove the conflict from the story, you're just left with words on a page. If the Hai are fat happy squirrels with no personality and always get along, then there won't be a meaningful Hai story.
